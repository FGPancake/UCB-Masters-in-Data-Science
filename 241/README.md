# MIDS 241 Final Project
Nutrition Experiment Using Source Cues final project for DATASCI 241

## Project Overview
We conducted a randomized experiment to test whether expert recommendations are perceived as more credible when compared to non-experts, specifically in the context of social media posts about nutrition. We believed expert source cues would lead individuals to judge expert recommendations as more credible when compared against other influencer source cues.

We set up the experiment to correctly isolate the treatment effect of the source of the recommendation (influencer vs expert) while accounting for participant bias. We surveyed 250 individuals and employed a within-subjects design, showing one randomized nutrition-related treatment post from an expert and one randomized nutrition-related control post from an influencer. We mixed in two dummy posts to distract participants from the goal of our survey. To more accurately measure treatment effect, we also asked pre-treatment questions to determine baseline accuracy, authenticity, believability, and trustworthiness, measuring those responses on a 7-point Likert scale both before and after respondants saw the claims. To accurately measure the complier average causal effect (CACE), we narrowed down our population according to muliple attention and completion time checks. We also ran multiple checks (attrition checks, attention checks, randomization order checks, position checks) to evaluate bias in our results before reporting them out.

We ended up with four models, ranging from simple to most complex, measuring treatment effect while accounting for various covariates (baseline trustworthiness, healthcare engagement, demographic variables) and interaction terms. When measuring for each of the treatment claims, we found a treatment effect overall for the source cues, but it was not statistically significant in any model. Preconceived biases did influence overall credibility score though and were statistically significant predictors across all models, and they did explain some of the variation in our models. After running the models on each claim, we did also run a final pooled within-subject regression with more power than each individual claim (double the responses) and found a small but statistically significant treatment effect across all models.
